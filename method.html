Method

How the chart audit works

The Charts chapter reports what a colour-collision checker finds in real charts. This page says exactly how those numbers were made, what they show, and what they do not.

The question

In a random sample of public charts from one publisher, what share contain at least one pair of colours that the checker flags as collapsing for each type of colour vision?

How the sample was drawn

  1. Frame. Every chart listed in the Our World in Data sitemap (4,292 charts), minus the 27 already used to try the checker.
  2. Draw. The list was shuffled with a seeded generator (seed 20261119). We walked down it, fetching each chart's PNG, until 120 were kept. Only 1 was skipped.
  3. Instrument. The checker's files and thresholds were written to a frozen snapshot before any of the 120 was analysed, and the analysis refuses to run if they change.
  4. Analysis. Fixed in advance: share of charts with at least one flagged pair, overall and by type of colour vision and by chart kind, with Wilson 95% intervals. Nothing was tuned afterwards.

What the checker does

It reads the pixels of a chart, finds the main colours used for categories, simulates what each colour looks like under protanopia, deuteranopia and tritanopia, and flags a pair when two colours that were clearly different become nearly the same and lightness cannot tell them apart. Each flag is graded: nearly identical when the simulated colour difference is under 13, otherwise harder to tell apart. It runs entirely in your browser; the same code powers the palette studio.

Limits: please keep these with any figure you quote

  1. The checker has not been validated against people with colour vision deficiency. A flag is a warning, not proof that a reader fails.
  2. One publisher, one house style. This is not “all charts”.
  3. The checker reads pixels. Sequential map scales and dense charts may be over-flagged, which is why results are split by chart kind.
  4. Whether a direct label or another second cue rescues a flagged pair is not measured. Many charts label their lines directly.
